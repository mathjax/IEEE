SECTION I.

Introduction

By the mid 1970s, comprehensive mathematical models of step motor systems, e.g., Singh [1] , had been developed and were in use in industry. Except for a phenomenon known as mid-frequency instability (MFI), the fundamental behavior of step motor systems was well understood.

MFI is characterized by a speed above which any small disturbance results in an oscillation that grows in amplitude. Just above the instability boundary, the oscillation often reaches a limiting amplitude without causing loss of synchronism. As speed is increased further, the oscillation continues to grow in amplitude until the motor stalls.

The dynamical equations of a step motor are nonlinear and time-varying (NLTV). As a result, the efforts to characterize dynamic stability have focused on deriving equivalent linear time-invariant (LTI) models, from which the characteristic roots, or eigenvalues, may be obtained.

In 1979 [2] , the fourth-order NLTV equations of the 2-phase permanent-magnet hybrid (PMH) step motor were solved at constant speed and linearized about the constant-speed solution. A particular solution was then found; and the characteristic equation was determined, which enabled stability analysis. This provided theoretical insight into MFI that had been lacking. In 1983 [3] , a simplified approach for the 2-phase PMH step motor was described for linearizing the fourth-order NLTV equations about a constant-speed solution and then transforming the resulting linear time-varying (LTV) system into a fourth-order LTI system.

Then, in 1984 [4] , the fifth-order NLTV equations of the 3-phase variable-reluctance (VR) step motor were solved at constant speed by first applying a transformation of variables; and then linearizing the resulting fourth-order NLTV system about the constant-speed solution, resulting in a fourth-order LTI system. State-space conventions were employed in the formulation of equations; and the transformation of variables was based on Park’s transformation [5] , modified to account for the tooth structure and resulting permeance model of the VR step motor. This approach, which was necessary for the VR step motor due to its angle-dependent inductance, was subsequently shown to be applicable to the 2-phase PMH step motor [6] , and to simplify the approach compared to [2] and [3] .

The mathematical model assumed in [2] , [3] , and [4] is of the type described by Kuo [7, pp. 1–113] . This type of model is also assumed in [6] , via reference to [2] .

The primary objective of this paper is to provide a model structure for applying the method of [4] to a broad range of step motors. Another objective is to provide a comprehensive generalized step motor model formulated in state-space representation, for the analysis and computer simulation of step motor systems.

SECTION III.

Physical Structure

A. Step Motor Structures

For any type of step motor, some form of geometric misalignment of teeth in the stator and rotor is necessary for torque production. This is typically attained by choosing from one of the following three motor structure classes:

  1. Uniform rotor tooth, stator pole, and stator tooth distributions with unequal stator and rotor tooth pitches

  2. Uniform rotor tooth and stator pole distributions, with non-uniformly distributed stator teeth arranged in subgroups at each pole, the tooth pitch within each subgroup being equal to the rotor tooth pitch

  3. Uniform rotor tooth distribution and non-uniform stator pole distribution, with non-uniformly distributed stator teeth arranged in subgroups at each pole, the tooth pitch within each subgroup being equal to the rotor tooth pitch

Step motors with class one structure, e.g., Snowdon and Madsen [8, Fig. 1] , and Kuo [9, table 1-1] have been described in the literature as have step motors with class two and three structures. For example, Chai [10, pg. 399] describes a 2-phase PMH step motor with class two structure and Heine [11] describes two types of 5-phase PMH step motors, one with class two structure and one with class three structure.

The core model developed in this paper is applicable to class one and class two structures. The derivation in Sections IV-A through IV-C is for class one structures, with the corresponding derivation for class two structures provided in Appendix A . The resulting flux linkage equations are the same in both cases, making possible the development of one dynamic stability model applicable to any $n$ -phase class one and class two step motor for which \begin{align*} n:\begin{cases} =2, \text {or}\ge 3\hskip .3em\text {and odd},&\text {PMH step motor}\\ \ge 3 \hskip .3em\text {and odd},&\text {VR step motor}. \end{cases}\end{align*} View Source \begin{align*} n:\begin{cases} =2, \text {or}\ge 3\hskip .3em\text {and odd},&\text {PMH step motor}\\ \ge 3 \hskip .3em\text {and odd},&\text {VR step motor}. \end{cases}\end{align*}

The core model developed in this paper is not applicable, per se, to class three structures due to their non-uniform stator pole distribution. It may be possible, however, to use it as a proxy to attain useful approximate results.

A. Type-Independent Flux Equation

1) Idealized Model Assumptions

Computer simulations show that MFI occurs and matches step motor behavior in the most basic step motor model. Therefore, the following simplifying assumptions are made:

  • The flux produced by the current flowing in the winding of any stator pole is sinusoidally distributed (spatially) about that pole

  • The air gap permeance of each stator tooth varies sinusoidally with rotor position

  • Eddy-currents, hysteresis, and magnetic saturation effects are neglected

Neglecting eddy-currents and hysteresis is realistic for machines with laminated cores, as the iron losses are minimal. Magnetic saturation does occur in most step motors, but primarily at lower speeds where current levels are highest. In the mid-speed range where MFI occurs, current levels are typically well below rated levels, and thus the effects of saturation are minimal.

D. Parameter Determination

In practice, better simulation results are attained when the lumped parameters $L_{0}$ , $L_{1}$ , and $K_{e}$ are determined from actual tests of the step motor rather than deriving them from the motor design parameters $N_{p}$ , $P_{f}$ , $P_{v}$ , $N_{s}$ , and $N_{r}$ . Inductances $L_{0}$ and $L_{1}$ are determined using an incremental inductance bridge or inductance meter, and $K_{e}$ is determined by driving the step motor externally at constant speed and measuring the open-circuit back EMF [7, ch. 3.7] .

SECTION XIII.

Effect of Constant-Current Drive on Dynamic Stability

The primary focus of this paper is the dynamic stability of step motors driven by resistance-limited constant-voltage drive. This is the simplest and least expensive driver solution and is adequate in many applications.

Another type of driver is the constant-current or chopper driver. In this drive scheme, the winding current of each energized phase is regulated to a fixed value, usually the rated current of the motor. The constant-current driver is well suited for higher performance applications, but it requires a significantly higher power supply voltage and a more complex drive circuit, both of which increase costs.

Pollack [14] examined the effect of sinusoidal constant-current drive on the 2-phase PMH step motor. He assumed that the phase currents could be algabraically specified at each instant in time, which eliminated the two dynamic equations governing the buildup of current in the windings. This idealized drive assumption reduced the order of the dynamic system from fourth-order to second-order and as a result, he found that the motor is stable at all speeds and does not experience MFI. In this section, his finding is extended to the generalized step motor.

SECTION XVI.

Conclusion

This paper has shown that it is possible to determine the dynamic performance and stability of a broad range of step motor types and configurations using a single analytical method, based on a comprehensive generalized step motor model.

Examples were given showing that the dynamic performance and stability are accurately predicted for constant speed operation including zero speed settling. Computer experiments comparing square-wave drive with sine-wave drive for both PMH and VR step motors indicated that the harmonic content of the square-wave excitation has minimal effect on MFI, thus validating the key modeling assumption in Section XII-A of sinusoidal excitation.

The effect of ideal constant-current drive was also investigated analytically and shown to eliminate MFI completely. Results of computer experiments using chopper drive did not quite match the those predicted for ideal constant-current drive but did show dramatic improvement versus constant-voltage drive.

In summary, the main contributions of this paper are 1) a generalized fourth-order LTI step motor dynamic stability model; and 2) the generalized ( $n+2$ )th -order NLTV step motor model upon which the dynamic stability model is based. Both models are applicable to any $n$ -phase class one or class two step motor for which: $n=2$ or $n\ge 3$ and odd (PMH step motor); or $n\ge 3$ and odd (VR step motor).
